A learned detector for wavetracker

How a small neural network can read the spectrogram the way you do when you correct tracks by eye: by looking at whole lines, their harmonics and their history at once. This page explains the design we chose after the literature search, with real benchmark data and a few things you can play with.

The picture the detector has to read

Below is one minute of a dev mixture from the dense benchmark (ladder-30-s0, 60 to 120 s, 340 to 500 Hz). Thirty fish are in the recording; this band holds eight of them plus the harmonics of fish further down. The image is the electrode-summed power spectrogram at the resolution wavetracker uses today: 0.61 Hz bins and a 1.64 s Hann window moved in 0.164 s steps. Turn on the overlays to compare it with the truth and with what the classic detector (the dense-linked run) found.

On the benchmark, the misses and errors of the classic pipeline fall into four groups. Every design decision below is aimed at one of them.

  • (a) Faint fish. Of the missed frames, 56 % (lab) and 72 % (field noise) have an SNR below 6 dB. 15 % and 27 % of all truth frames are below the noise and cannot be recovered by anything. measured
  • (b) Close fish. 32 % and 20 % of misses are within 3 Hz of another line. Two fish often make one spectral peak. measured
  • (c) False lines. Harmonics of low fish and stray lines become long false identities once the tracker connects them. measured
  • (d) Fragmentation. One fish split into many identities, which the linker only partly repairs. measured

The hand-made fixes have saturated. Halving the bin width to 0.31 Hz gave +0.05 recall but doubled the false lines and left IDF1 where it was. A track-guided faint-peak acceptor raised field IDF1 from 0.613 to 0.649, and its false additions extended false lines. measured Adding peaks without looking at their context does not help identities.

What changes and what stays

Only the detector is replaced. Today it finds peaks in each frame and groups them into a fundamental plus harmonics with fixed rules. The learned detector replaces both steps with one network that looks at a whole patch of the spectrogram and says, for every time–frequency bin, how likely it is that a fish's fundamental is there. Everything after the detections stays: the tracker, the fragment linker, the results format and the eodsorter GUI. The old detector stays selectable with detector.method: classic | learned, and the benchmark baselines keep pinning classic.

Why keep the tracker instead of letting a network also decide identity? Every learned identity method we found handles a handful of sources at most: two speakers, four choir voices. They fail exactly where the sources share a range. published In multi-object tracking of near-identical dancers, adding learned appearance features made association worse, while better motion models helped. published Same-species fish a few Hz apart are the extreme version of that. The electrode pattern, which wavetracker already uses, behaves like a slowly changing position, and it already beats frequency on hard assignment conflicts (99.57 % vs 94.83 % in Raab et al. 2022). published So the network's job is better detections with honest scores, and identity stays where it works.

Salience: a map instead of rules

The network outputs a salience map: one number between 0 and 1 for every time–frequency bin, meaning "a fish's fundamental is here". It is trained like an image segmentation net. The target for each frame is zero everywhere except for a small bump at each true fundamental. Peaks in the output become detections. This is the "deep salience" design from music multi-pitch estimation (Bittner et al. 2017). published

The bump width has to be set in Hz. Music models use pitch-relative bumps that fall to zero within a quarter tone. At 800 Hz that is ±23 Hz, which would swallow every neighbour of a fish. Our bumps are Gaussians with σ ≈ 0.6 Hz (one bin). Where two fish are close, the targets are combined by taking the maximum, not the sum, so two bumps stay two bumps. our reasoning

Besides the salience, the first version gets a sub-bin offset: a second output that says how far the true frequency lies from the bin centre, trained only where a fish is (as in CenterNet, Zhou et al. 2019). published Later versions add a "line but not a fundamental" score for harmonics, hum and stray lines, and a count of fish per bin (0, 1, 2+) for the close-pair case. Peaks are picked along frequency with a low threshold. The salience value travels with each detection, so the tracker can treat confident and doubtful detections differently. The threshold itself must be tuned on dev, separately for lab and field noise: deep salience's best threshold was 0.3 on its validation set and 0.05 on another dataset. published

The harmonic stack, rebuilt for a linear axis

A plain CNN has a hard time with harmonics. A fish at 410 Hz has its 2nd harmonic 410 Hz higher; a fish at 640 Hz has it 640 Hz higher. No fixed-size kernel sees a fundamental and its harmonics together. Music models fix this with the harmonic stack: they build several copies of the spectrum, each read at a multiple of the frequency, and put them on top of each other as input channels. Then channel h at position f holds the power at h·f, and a small kernel at position f sees the whole harmonic series of a candidate fundamental at once. Removing the stack "substantially reduces performance across all metrics and datasets" in Basic Pitch. published

Music does this on a log-frequency axis with 20-cent bins. Those are 11.6 Hz wide at 1 kHz, far too coarse for fish 3 Hz apart. Keeping 0.61 Hz on a log axis up to 2 kHz would need about 11,500 bins instead of 3,180. our reasoning On a linear axis that starts at 0 Hz the stack is simpler: harmonic h of bin k is exactly bin h·k. So the stack is an index lookup, with a small maximum over about h neighbouring bins because a fish rarely sits on a bin centre. our reasoning The channels f/2 and f/3 look below the candidate. A real fundamental has nothing there; a harmonic has its own fundamental there.

Two fish, one peak

Failure mode (b) is about physics more than rules, so it helps to see what two close fish do to the spectrogram. A Hann window of 1.64 s gives each fish a main lobe about 2.4 Hz wide. Two equally strong fish separate into two peaks only when they are more than about 1.2 Hz apart; a weaker fish needs more distance before it shows up as more than a shoulder. Closer than that, the two fish add up inside one lobe, and their relative phase turns once every 1/Δf seconds. The bin's power rises and falls with that period: a beat. Frames come every 0.164 s, so beats up to 3 Hz are sampled without aliasing. our reasoning

Two consequences shape the design. First, for pairs between roughly 1.2 and 3 Hz apart, the information is already in a single frame's spectrum as two lobes or a shoulder. The classic peak picker loses it; a network that sees the full lobe shape can learn to keep it. Second, below about 1.2 Hz only two cues remain: the beat over time, and the electrodes. Each fish has its own amplitude pattern across the grid, so the beat is deep on electrodes where both fish are similarly strong and shallow where one dominates. We will check that this cue survives real SNRs before building anything for it (stage 3).

The network, layer by layer

The network is a fully convolutional net that never pools or strides along frequency, so its output keeps the full 0.61 Hz resolution. Deep salience, PESTO and the dolphin-whistle network all avoid frequency pooling for the same reason: pooling throws away exactly the position information that separates close pitches. published All sizes are set in Hz and seconds and converted to bins and frames from the spectrogram settings, which are stored with the model.

Why time context recovers faint fish

A fish line lasts minutes to hours, but noise in a bin changes from window to window. Averaging the evidence along a line over n independent windows shrinks the noise by √n while the line stays. That is how you spot a faint fish by eye: you follow it. The cleanest published number for the value of time context comes from piano transcription: removing the recurrent layers from Onsets and Frames dropped frame F1 from 78.3 to 67.6. published The widget below averages a synthetic spectrogram along time. Try a faint SNR and a long window, then look at what happens to the rise at 25 s.

GRU and attention in two paragraphs

A GRU reads a sequence one step at a time and carries a small memory vector forward. At each step a learned gate decides how much of the old memory to keep and how much of the new input to write. Run it once forward and once backward along time, separately for every frequency bin with shared weights, and each bin's output knows what happened at that frequency over the last and next tens of seconds. Dilated 1-D convolutions reach the same span by skipping frames: kernels with dilation 1, 2, 4, 8 … double their reach every layer.

Attention is a learned weighted average whose weights depend on content. Every element emits a query ("what I am looking for"), a key ("what I contain") and a value ("what I pass on"). Element i's output is the average of all values, weighted by how well its query matches each key. A convolution mixes a fixed neighbourhood with fixed weights; attention picks what to mix. We do not need it for the first versions. It becomes an option for the temporal module, and for letting the summed stream ask the electrodes "which of you see this peak?".

Electrodes: summed for finding, separate for separating

The summed spectrogram is sparse and keeps lines gap-free, which is why it finds the ridges. It throws away where each fish is. Here is the close group from the crop above (372–392 Hz), electrode by electrode, in the 3×3 grid layout of the recording.

The branch has to work for 2 electrodes on a boat, 9 in the benchmark and 16 or more on field grids, with any ordering. The pattern that does this in speech separation with ad-hoc microphone arrays is TAC (transform, average, concatenate; Luo et al. 2020). The same small network processes every electrode. The only operation across electrodes is a symmetric one, an average and a maximum, which does not care how many electrodes there are or in which order they come. published With 2, 4 and 6 microphones, adding TAC raised separation from 9.0, 8.9 and 9.5 dB to 9.8, 11.2 and 11.7 dB, and stacking channels in a fixed order scored worse than averaging them (6.9 vs 8.7 dB). published VarArray (Yoshioka et al. 2022) feeds each microphone the magnitude of the array average plus that microphone's phase relative to it, which is the "summed stream plus per-electrode detail" idea in published form. published

Why there is no separate max-over-electrodes channel

A fish seen on only a few electrodes is diluted in the sum. A per-bin maximum across electrodes looks like a fix, but it also takes the maximum of the noise. We simulated a fish on k of N electrodes and measured how well each pooling separates it from noise (AUC, 1 is perfect). our simulation

On single frames the maximum gains nothing, as you suspected. It helps only after each electrode's evidence has been averaged over time, and only for fish seen on a few electrodes; for fish on many electrodes the sum wins. The electrode branch integrates over time per electrode before it pools, and pools with both mean and max, so it gets the benefit where it exists without a separate channel.

One more physical cue may matter later. At EOD frequencies the field in water is quasi-static, so for a single fish all electrodes are in phase or in antiphase. Two fish in one bin break that pattern. Power spectrograms discard phase, but the training data will keep it, so signed or complex electrode inputs can be tried as an ablation. our reasoning

Training data: mixed as signals, with honest labels

In every field that measured it, the training data decided more than the architecture. Dolphin-whistle networks trained on synthetic contours placed into real ocean noise came within 0.09 F1 of fully supervised training. published A domain-randomised bioacoustic detector lost F1 when its training was limited to high-SNR events (0.445 to 0.402). published For us that means real field noise, an SNR range that reaches well below 0 dB, and mixtures that keep beats.

The benchmark already builds mixtures from real two-fish tube recordings. For training we make new mixtures on the fly, on the GPU, from raw traces. Nothing but the raw traces and their truth is stored, so every feature (phase, beats, the full band) stays available.

The source split changes. Today's train mixtures reuse the dev sources. A linker can live with that, but a network trained on thousands of mixtures could learn the individual fish and inflate every dev score. The sources are re-split by recording group: train is 2019-g3 plus 2022-p1 to p3 (20 trials), dev is 2019-g4 plus 2022-p4 (10 trials), and test stays as it is. The classic detector is re-scored on the new dev.

Masks: three truth defects, three treatments

The loss is computed per bin and multiplied by a mask. Getting the mask wrong teaches the wrong thing, and this has been measured: whistle networks trained naively on labels made by the old tracker scored worse than that tracker (F1 74.1 vs 75.95), while a loss that respects missing labels reached 86.3, close to full supervision (87.5). published

Tracking with scores

A detector that finds faint fish also produces more doubtful detections. If the tracker treats them all alike, it either misses the faint fish (high threshold) or builds false identities out of clutter (low threshold). ByteTrack (Zhang et al. 2022) resolves this with two thresholds: new tracks start only from confident detections, but existing tracks may absorb weak detections near where they are expected. Applied to nine trackers, this raised IDF1 by 1 to 10 points. published We add it to wavetracker's tracker early, behind config, so that the classic detector can use it too and the comparison stays fair. Classic peaks get a stand-in score from their peak SNR.

The fragment linker was trained on fragments from the classic detector. It is retrained on fragments from the learned one, with mean salience as an extra feature. Learned identity cues (an embedding per detection, or a windowed linker in the style of Trackastra) are a later, optional step, kept only if an ablation shows an IDF1 gain.

Measuring it

Music metrics count a pitch as correct within half a semitone. At 800 Hz that is ±23 Hz, which would credit a detection to any of several neighbouring fish. We match detections to truth one-to-one per frame with tolerances of 0.6, 1.2 and 2.5 Hz.

Every number is reported per stratum, so that each failure mode has its own figure:

  • recall by SNR band, with the frames below the noise reported apart and neither rewarded nor punished;
  • recall by distance to the nearest other line (< 1.2, 1.2–3, > 3 Hz), the split the window physics suggests;
  • recall on rises separately, to catch over-smoothing;
  • false positives by type: harmonic of a truth fish, hum, known real line missing from the truth, other;
  • after tracking and linking: IDF1, HOTA, fragments per fish, and the number and duration of false identities.

Two oracle runs show what is left to gain: truth detections through the tracker and linker (the ceiling for any detector), and learned detections with perfect association (the ceiling for any tracker). All development uses dev. Test is scored once at the end.

Plan: light first, then earn the rest

The first version has one job: show on dev whether a small learned detector beats the hand-tuned one. Heavy parts come only after that.

  1. Harness

    New source split and rebuilt dev mixtures; stratified scoring with typed false positives and the two oracle runs; classic re-scored on the new dev; two-threshold association behind config; the time-domain mixer and the loss masks; detector.method: classic | learned stub.

  2. Light salience network

    Summed stream, harmonic stack, the six-layer CNN (about 0.08 M parameters), salience and offset heads, masked BCE. Trained on the existing 20-minute segments with on-the-fly mixing and field noise. Expected training time about an hour or two on the RTX 4080 estimate.

  3. Go or stop

    Compare with classic on the new dev, lab and field noise, through the same tracker and linker: recall below 6 dB, the 1.2–3 Hz stratum, false-identity time and IDF1. Continue only if the learned detector wins without more false identities.

  4. Context and generality

    Temporal module over 30–60 s, focal loss, wider warp and per-fish harmonic-profile augmentation, truth for the full 6 h of the train trials, retrained linker.

  5. Electrode branch

    First a cheap check without learning: do two fish in one bin show up in the electrode covariance at real SNRs, split by Δf below and above 1.2 Hz? If yes, the TAC branch with mean+max pooling, zero-initialised gate and a fish-count head.

  6. Later, if they pay

    Identity embeddings and a windowed linker; 16+ electrodes via a virtual grid; signed or complex electrode inputs; self-training on the unlabelled field archive.

Decisions so far

  • Identity stays with the tracker and linker.
  • Training data keeps every feature: raw traces mixed in the time domain, spectrograms computed per batch.
  • No separate max-over-electrodes channel.
  • Two-threshold association comes early, for both detectors.
  • Sources re-split by group; full trial hours once the first version shows a gain.
  • 16+ electrodes later. Other species and frequencies through a wider warp and harmonic-profile augmentation.
  • Evaluation on the dense benchmark only; test once at the end.

Main risks

  • Few distinct fish. Twenty train trials of one species may not generalise; a learning curve over the number of trials will show whether more recordings would pay.
  • Mask errors leak both ways: a missed residual line becomes a hidden negative, a masked harmonic stops being one.
  • Time context can also carry a line through noise into a false identity. False-identity time is watched at every stage.
  • The electrode cue for pairs closer than 1.2 Hz is untested, and close fish often swim close together, where spatial cues fade.
